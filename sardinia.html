Getting your bearings

Alghero is a walled seaside town of about 45,000 people on the north-west coast. It was Catalan for three centuries, so the old town has Spanish-looking towers, street signs in Catalan, and a paella on most menus. The whole historic centre is maybe fifteen minutes end to end on foot. The are the town's living room: a raised stone promenade above the water, lined with bars, facing directly west. That west-facing part matters — the sun sets into the sea in front of you at about 19:25 in late September.

The airport is at , 10 km north, a small 1930s planned village with a quiet marina. Between Fertilia and town are the easy family beaches, first, and a flat cycle path that connects them to the old town.

Keep going west and you enter the Porto Conte regional park: a big sheltered bay, pine woods, and a white limestone headland called that drops 200 m straight into the sea. This is the postcard image of the area. Inside the park are the best beaches (, , ), a sea cave, a 3,000-year-old stone village, an animal reserve, and two easy clifftop walks. Almost everything I'd suggest doing is in here, within 25 minutes of your hotel.

South of town the coast turns wild. One road, the SP105, runs 45 km along empty cliffs to , a river town of coloured houses under a ruined castle. It is the one trip worth leaving the area for.

The headland: caves, animals and cliffs

The is a sea cave at the foot of Capo Caccia, with a salt lake inside and a lot of stalactites. There are two ways in. By land you drive to the cape, park at La Stalla, take a shuttle, and walk down 654 steps cut into the cliff — spectacular, but the climb back up in the sun is hard work for a seven-year-old, and the land slot must be booked. By sea, a boat leaves from the , sails under the cliffs past a pierced islet called Foradada, and lands you at the cave mouth. The boat costs about €17 and the cave entry is separate (roughly €14–18 for adults, less for children) — people constantly get caught out by that. I'd take the boat: it's the boat trip and the cave in one morning, and nobody has to climb anything.

For the cliffs without the cave, there is a free clifftop viewpoint at the . It faces west and is one of the better sunsets on the island. Bring a jacket; it's windy up there.

The surprise of this trip is likely to be , also called Noah's Ark — a former prison farm turned wildlife reserve on the north side of the headland. Albino donkeys from Asinara island, fallow deer, small wild Giara horses, wild boar, and reintroduced griffon vultures riding the updrafts over the cliffs. At the gate there is an information hut, a playground, picnic tables, bike hire, and four-seat electric buggies from about €20 an hour, which you should reserve ahead because they run out. Entry is a couple of euros a head; last admission is well before the 18:00 closing, so go in the morning.

Next door, is a flat headland walk through pines to abandoned World War II gun positions and bunkers, with Capo Caccia filling the view across the bay. Easy underfoot, genuinely exciting if you are seven.

And in the middle of all this, right on the main road, sits . Sardinia has around 7,000 nuraghi — dry-stone towers built roughly 1500 BC by a civilisation that left no writing. Palmavera is a tower plus the remains of a whole village of round huts, and you can climb inside it. Half an hour, €7, and it explains the entire island.

Bosa, and whether it's worth the drive

Yes, mostly for the road. The SP105 hugs the cliffs for 45 km with almost nothing built on it — about 1h15 each way, slow and twisty, and griffon vultures nest along it. At the end, Bosa sits on the Temo, the only navigable river in Sardinia: pastel houses stacked up the hill, a stone bridge, and the ruined on top with a small church inside covered in 14th-century frescoes. Entry is around €5–6.50; the climb up through the old quarter is steep but short.

Down at the river mouth, has a long shallow beach, and small boats run short trips up the river from the quay in town. A full day, not a half day.

If you'd rather swap it: La Pelosa at Stintino, an hour north, is the famous shallow turquoise beach that looks Caribbean. It needs a paid booking (€3.50 per adult, under-12s free, only through spiaggialapelosa.it) until 15 October, and after 18:00 you can walk in free. It's the better beach; Bosa is the better day.

Wine and very old stones

Ten minutes inland, is one of the largest single wine estates in Europe, founded in 1899 by two Piedmontese who drained the marsh here. The guided tour walks the old cellars and a small museum, ends with a tasting, and runs Monday to Friday by booking only (visit@sellaemosca.com). Their Vermentino and Torbato are what you'll be drinking all week anyway.

While they were planting vines in 1903 they dug up , the largest prehistoric necropolis in Sardinia: 38 tombs cut into soft rock around 3000 BC, some with carved bull horns. It's a field of holes in the ground you can crawl into, five minutes from the winery, and there's a combined ticket with Nuraghe Palmavera.

The car question

You don't have to decide the way most people do. Rentals here are small local outfits, several of them in the old town, several will deliver the car to your hotel, and short rentals are normal. Four honest options:

Option A

Airport, on arrival, all five days

Maximum freedom, one queue, and you can drive straight to the hotel.

  • You land around 21:10 — confirm in writing that the desk covers your flight. lists 08:00–23:00, and is staffed 24h and delivers.
  • Old-town parking is paid and tight; check your hotel has a space before you book this.
  • Bring a real credit card in the driver's name — several agencies still insist.
Option B — my suggestion

Pick up in town on Sunday morning

Skip driving tired in the dark. Taxi in on Saturday night, walk Sunday, collect the car when you want it.

  • is on a square in the old town and open 09:00–20:30 every day; and are a short walk away and both take WhatsApp bookings.
  • Four days instead of five, and no airport surcharge.
  • Ask for a child seat when you book — most of them have one.
Option C

Two days only

Take the car just for Bosa and for the headland day, and live on foot and bikes the rest of the time.

  • Cheapest sensible version. Town agencies rent by the day and several deliver to your hotel.
  • Works because the cave is reached by boat, and the beaches are reachable by bike or bus.
  • Risk: book the two days now — mid-September is still busy.
Option D

No car at all

Possible, with one real sacrifice.

  • ARST bus line 9321 runs Alghero–Capo Caccia all year; a seasonal Beach Bus covers the beaches into September. Check both timetables the week before.
  • The cave is a boat trip from the town quay, so it needs no car at all.
  • Bosa becomes a long, awkward bus day — you'd drop it, or take a guided tour.

On the map, car pins are pale blue. The big international desk at the airport has noticeably worse reviews than the small local agencies in town, most of which sit around 4.9 with hundreds of reviews. Whoever you pick, photograph the car all over before you leave.

Bikes

A paved cycle path runs from the port through town, out past Maria Pia, over the lagoon bridge to Fertilia, and on toward the beaches — flat almost the whole way. Alghero to Fertilia is about 5 km and genuinely easy for a child. Alghero to Le Bombarde is about 12 km each way with one real hill near the end; 24 km round trip after a beach afternoon is a lot for a seven-year-old, so either take a tag-along or trailer, or ride out and get a lift back. There are also gentle gravel tracks inside Le Prigionette and Punta Giglio, and bikes can be hired at the Prigionette gate itself.

Via Lido, near Maria Pia. Cheap city bikes — one review reports €16 for two days — helmets included, pick-up and drop-off offered. Best for the easy flat rides.

Via Majorca, in the old town. E-bikes, hybrids and guided rides; delivers to hotels. Recommended by the park itself. Closed Sundays.

Via Garibaldi. The serious end — road and e-bikes, open daily, will shuttle you and the bikes. Useful if one adult wants the Bosa coast road on two wheels.

Also Via Garibaldi. Bikes, scooters and cars from one small family shop — handy if you want a bike for three days and a car for two.

Ask specifically about a child's bike, a tag-along or a trailer when you book. Not every shop keeps them.

Five days, loosely

Treat these as building blocks. Only two things are time-sensitive: the cave boat wants a morning, and Sella & Mosca only runs Monday to Friday.

Saturday — arrival

Land ~21:10, 10 km to town. Eat late if anything is open; the old town serves until about 22:30.

Sunday — town

Old town lanes, the Coral Museum (closed 13:00–16:30), a swim at Maria Pia, and the sea walls at sunset. Collect the car in the morning if you chose Option B.

Monday — bikes and stones

Cycle path out to Fertilia, Nuraghe Palmavera on the way, beach at Le Bombarde. Or ride only to Fertilia and drive the rest.

Tuesday — boat day

Morning boat from the quay to the Grotta di Nettuno. Afternoon at Mugoni. Sunset from the Capo Caccia viewpoint if there's energy left.

Wednesday — Bosa

Coast road south, castle, coloured streets, river boat, beach at Bosa Marina, back in the golden hour rather than the dark.

Thursday — animals, then fly

Le Prigionette from 09:00, easy walk at Punta Giglio, lunch in Fertilia, evening flight. Or swap in Sella & Mosca and Anghelu Ruju, which are five minutes from the airport.

Book this week

  • The car, in whichever shape you choose — and the child seat with it.
  • Bikes, if you want e-bikes or anything child-sized.
  • The electric buggy at Le Prigionette, if you'd rather ride than walk the reserve.
  • Sella & Mosca by email, weekdays only.
  • La Pelosa, only if you decide to swap it for Bosa — official site only, tickets open 48h ahead for most slots.
